Every model launch comes with a chart of scores. This lesson is about reading those scores, and about what to trust when the scores and your own experience disagree.

By the end you will know what goes into a benchmark number, how the numbers go wrong, and how to build a small test of your own from work you have already shipped.

What "good" means

"Good" is five separate questions

A model can lead on hard math, be middling at following your team's code style, and be too slow or expensive for autocomplete.

Decide which of the five you care about before you look at a number.

Go deeper: good at what?

What most public benchmarks measure

ReliabilityEvery time, or one time in three?

"Is this model good" has no single answer. The screen splits it into five questions.

Capability asks whether the model can do the task at all. That is the highlighted row, and it is what most public benchmarks measure.

The other four are what you live with. Reliability is whether it works every time or one time in three. Fit is whether it works in your language, your framework and your tool. Cost and speed cover dollars, quota and waiting. Behavior covers staying in scope, admitting doubt, and writing code a reviewer can follow.

So a model can top a leaderboard and still be the wrong pick for your team. Decide which rows you care about first. Then go looking for evidence on those rows.

Ask each person to rank the five for their own work. Autocomplete users and people running overnight agents will rank them differently.

What a benchmark is

A score describes a whole setup

A benchmark is a fixed set of tasks plus a scoring rule. Running one takes six ingredients, and the model is only the first.

The harness is the program around the model that supplies tools and runs the loop. The grader decides pass or fail. Change any ingredient and the number moves.

A benchmark is a fixed set of tasks and a rule for scoring them. People read the result as a fact about the model. The diagram shows six things that go into it.

The model is one box. The harness is the program wrapped around the model. It sends the prompt, offers tools such as a shell, and decides when to stop. Settings cover reasoning effort and token budget. The task set has a version, and versions differ. The grader decides pass or fail. For code that usually means hidden unit tests. For writing it can be a person or another model acting as judge.

The last box is attempts. Most headline numbers are pass@1, the share of tasks solved on the first try.

When two scores differ, any of the six boxes could be the reason.

Put a launch chart on screen and ask the room to find all six ingredients in its footnotes. Usually two or three are missing.

Attempts

The same model can look 99% reliable or 51%

Models are stochastic, so one task can pass on one run and fail on the next. Two metrics count repeated attempts in opposite ways.

pass@k asks whether at least one of k attempts worked. pass^k asks whether all of them did. Check which one a score uses.

Run a model twice on one task and you can get a pass and a fail. Benchmarks handle that in two ways, and the two give very different pictures.

Take a model that solves a task 80 percent of the time, and give it three tries. The left panel is pass@3. It counts a pass if any one try works. The chance that all three fail is tiny, so the score is 99 percent.

The right panel is pass^3, written with a caret. It counts a pass only if all three tries work. That comes to 51 percent. Same model, same task.

Pick the metric that fits how you work. If you can check results cheaply and retry, pass@k is fair. If an agent runs unattended, you need pass^k. The τ-bench paper introduced it and found GPT-4o agents below 25 percent at eight tries in its retail domain.

Ask which metric fits an autocomplete tool, a PR-writing agent, and an unattended nightly job.

Contamination

The model may have seen the test

Models train on huge scrapes of the internet. If a benchmark's questions or solutions are online, a high score can reflect memory as well as problem-solving. That's contamination.

In a 2025 study, models saw only the text of a GitHub issue. They often named the file that needed fixing without ever seeing the repository.

Models learn from huge scrapes of the internet. Public benchmarks live on the internet. If the questions or their solutions were in the training data, a high score can reflect memory as well as skill. That is called contamination.

The bars come from a 2025 paper called The SWE-Bench Illusion. Models were shown only the text of a GitHub issue. They had no repository to look at. On tasks from SWE-bench Verified, they still named the file that needed fixing up to 76 percent of the time. On repositories outside the benchmark, the best was 53 percent.

You can also test for this by writing fresh questions. A 2024 study rewrote a grade-school math benchmark with new problems. Some model families lost up to 8 points.

Your own private code is less likely to be in anyone's training set, and that is one reason the last part of this lesson builds a test from it.

Ask how a model could name the right file without seeing the repo. Then ask what that says about its score on those tasks.

Shelf life

A benchmark stays useful for a year or two

When top models all score near the ceiling, a benchmark stops telling them apart. That's saturation. Add leaks into training data and wrong answer keys, and benchmarks keep getting retired.

Look for fresh, held-out, realistic tasks and a documented harness. The names will change.

Benchmarks wear out. The first way is saturation. Once the top models all score in the nineties, the test can no longer separate them. The top row lists benchmarks in that state.

The second way is leaking into training data, which the last screen covered. The third is bad answer keys. One study estimated that about six and a half percent of the questions in MMLU, a popular knowledge test, contain errors.

The middle row has the best-known case. OpenAI audited 138 SWE-bench Verified tasks that its o3 model didn't solve reliably. It found material flaws in the tests or descriptions of about 59 percent of them. In February 2026 it stopped reporting that benchmark.

The bottom row lists benchmarks that still separate frontier models as of November 2026. Expect that row to change. When you meet a benchmark you don't know, ask how old it is, whether its tasks are held out, and whether it documents the harness.

Watch for someone quoting a SWE-bench Verified score from a 2026 launch post as if it settled anything.

Benchmark-maxing

Once a score sells a model, labs aim at the score

Goodhart's law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

Benchmark-maxing is optimizing a model, or how it's presented, for scores at the expense of the behavior users get. It ranges from defensible to dishonest.

Go deeper: benchmark-maxing

  1. Benchmark-like training dataLegitimate skill-building, until the training set looks so much like the test that scores rise faster than ability.
  2. ContaminationTest items or solutions end up in training data, by accident or carelessness.
  3. Eval-specific tuningA variant tuned to please one grader or audience.
  4. Reward hackingTraining rewards "tests pass", so the model learns to special-case tests or over-engineer.
  5. Cherry-picked setupReport the agent, effort level and benchmark version that look best. Drop the benchmark that looked bad.
  6. Undisclosed best of NSeveral attempts or a majority vote, reported as one score.
  7. Selective private testingTest many variants in private and publish only the winner.

Goodhart's law says that when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Benchmark scores sell models, so labs aim at them.

The list shows seven ways that happens. The first is the most defensible. Labs train on tasks shaped like popular benchmarks. That builds real skill, up to the point where the practice set looks too much like the exam.

Number three has a well-known example. In April 2025, Meta's Llama 4 launch highlighted a second-place ranking on the Arena leaderboard. The ranked entry was an experimental variant tuned for conversation. It was a different model from the one Meta released. When the released model was tested, it ranked far lower.

Numbers five and six are the ones you can catch yourself. They live in the footnotes: the effort level, the number of attempts, and the benchmark that appeared in the last launch post and is missing from this one.

Ask the room where the line is. If a lab trains hard on coding tasks and gets better at coding benchmarks, is that benchmark-maxing?

The harness is part of the score. These bars come from a June 2026 study by the Terminal-Bench team. They ran each model twice. Once in its vendor's own command-line tool, and once in a neutral research harness called Terminus-2.

GPT-5.5 scored 24.7 percent in Codex and 21.2 in the neutral harness. Gemini 3.1 scored 12.2 percent in gemini-cli and 7.3 in the neutral one. Both models have been replaced since, so read the bars for the size of the harness effect.

Read the caption too. The authors say none of these gaps reached statistical significance. The next screen is about that problem.

Effort and token budget count as harness settings. A 2026 paper that varied them across twelve models concluded that benchmark scores depend on the protocol. More effort is no guarantee either. Reviewers reported Opus 5 doing worse at its highest effort settings.

So when a leaderboard lists a model, look for the agent next to it. Then test in the tool you use.

Ask which harness each person uses daily, and whether the last score they quoted came from that harness.

Error bars

A lead smaller than the error bar tells you little

A pass rate from a handful of tasks is a rough estimate. With 20 tasks and a 60% pass rate, the 95% interval is roughly plus or minus 22 points.

Two configurations at 55% and 65% on 20 tasks are statistically indistinguishable. Ask for the interval before you believe a gap.

Public leaderboards have the same problem at a smaller scale. On the Terminal-Bench 4.1 leaderboard on November 18, 2026, second place and fifth place were 1.9 points apart. Each score carried a 95 percent interval of about 3 points either way.

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 announcement, from September 2026, says benchmark margins have become a less reliable guide at these levels of capability.

More tasks shrink the range. Repeated runs help too. A later screen shows a third option, which is to compare on the same tasks and count disagreements.

Ask how many tasks it would take to trust a 10-point gap. Then work out the standard error together for 100 tasks.

Reading a claim

Ask eight questions of any score

Most of the answers are in the footnotes. A missing answer is information too.

Go deeper: the checklist

"Model X scores 72% on Benchmark Y"

  1. Which version?Different versions have different tasks.
  2. Which harness?The lab's agent, a vendor CLI, or a neutral one.
  3. What settings?Effort, token budget, number of attempts.
  4. Who ran it?The lab, the maintainers, or an independent evaluator.
  5. Compared with what?Rivals run the same way, or quoted from their own posts.
  6. What's the error bar?
  7. What's missing?Benchmarks reported last time and dropped this time.
  8. What does each task cost?A higher score at three times the cost may be the worse buy.
Illustrative claim

This is the checklist to run on any benchmark claim. Take the sentence at the top: Model X scores 72 percent on Benchmark Y.

The first three questions are about the setup. Which version of the benchmark, which harness, and what settings. A footnote that says "max effort, best of five" changes what the number means.

Question four is who ran it. Question five is what it was compared with. On Terminal-Bench, Anthropic's September 2026 Opus 5.5 post compares its model at extra-high effort against a rival's own reported figure at high effort. It says so in a footnote, and that footnote is the kind of thing you are looking for.

Six is the error bar. Seven is what's missing. If a lab reported a benchmark last time and dropped it this time, ask why. Eight is cost per task. Many 2026 launch posts now chart score against cost for that reason.

Hand out a real launch post. Give the room five minutes to answer all eight from its text and footnotes, and count how many can't be answered.

Second opinions

Independent evaluators run every model the same way

A launch post comes from the lab, which picks the benchmarks and the settings. Independent evaluators give you consistency: one setup for every model.

They still choose the benchmarks and harnesses, and some have commercial ties to labs. Use two or three as a cross-check.

Time horizon is a trend line, with a big asterisk

METR measures the 50% time horizon: the length of task, in skilled-human time, that a model completes half the time.

It has grown fast. The 80% horizon is much shorter, and METR's tasks are cleaner and better specified than most real tickets.

One benchmark you will hear quoted a lot is METR's time horizon. It asks how long a task a model can finish, measured by how long the task takes a skilled person. The headline figure is the length the model completes half the time.

The top three bars show the growth. About 50 minutes in early 2025. About 320 minutes by January 2026. About 12 hours for the public frontier in February and March of 2026.

Now read the small print. The range on that 12-hour figure runs from 5 hours to 61. And the dark bar at the bottom is the same models at an 80 percent success rate. That horizon is about an hour and a half.

METR also reviewed the successful runs on its longest tasks and judged at least 16 percent of them illegitimate. The model had cheated.

Use this number as a trend. It says agents are getting better at long work, quickly. It doesn't promise that your eight-hour ticket will come back done.

Ask which bar you would plan a project around: the one that works half the time or the one that works four times in five.

Productivity studies

Studies of developer speed run from 56% less time to 19% more

Benchmarks measure models. Teams care whether people with models ship faster, and the experiments disagree.

They measured different tasks, people, tools and outcomes. Greenfield tasks and less experienced developers show the biggest gains. Experts in their own mature repos show the least.

Ask which study looks most like the room's daily work. Most people will pick the one that matches what they already believed.

Self-report

People feel faster than they measure

In METR's 2025 study, developers predicted AI would make them 24% faster. Afterward they believed it had made them 20% faster. They were 19% slower.

Surveys of how fast people feel aren't measured time savings. If you survey your team, pair it with something measured: cycle time, golden-task timing, or change-failure rate.

Ask everyone to write down how much faster AI makes them. Then ask who has timed it.

Vibes

Vibes are data once you write them down

Experienced users often spot a change before benchmarks do. Their impressions are also noisy: a new model feels smarter for a week, and you remember the one spectacular failure over forty quiet successes.

Keep a log, one line per notable result. Decide what you're testing before you look, and judge again after two weeks.

Illustrative

Vibes are the impressions of people who use a model every day. They are real evidence. In April 2025, OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update that had become too flattering. It wrote afterward that some expert testers had said the model felt off before launch, and the positive test metrics had won the argument. Its lesson was to give that kind of signal more weight.

Vibes are also noisy. New models get a honeymoon week. One viral post sets everyone's expectations. Memory keeps the disaster and drops the forty quiet wins.

The fix is on screen: a log. One line each time something notable happens, with the date, the model, the harness and version, the kind of task, and what happened. Then you count lines when you'd otherwise be recalling.

Two more habits. Hide the model names before you judge output, because people favor the model they expect to win. And prefer specific reports. Two entries in this log say the same specific thing about Model A. That is worth more than a general feeling.

Ask who keeps any record of model failures. Start a shared log for the class today.

Your own eval

Your best benchmark is your own merged PRs

A golden task is a real, solved piece of your work, frozen so you can replay it. Start with 10 to 20 from the last 3 to 12 months and grow toward 30 to 50.

Mix bug fixes, small features, refactors and tests. Include one trap, where the right move is to ask a question or refuse.

Your own eval

Score each run blind, against a rubric

Hidden tests cover correctness. A rubric covers what tests miss: scope, code quality, and whether the summary is honest.

Replace model names with random IDs and shuffle before anyone scores. Also record minutes, human interventions, and cost or quota used.

Passing tests is the start of grading. The rubric on screen has ten points. Four go to correctness. Two go to scope, meaning the change is about as small as the reference fix. Two go to code quality. One each goes to adding a real regression test and to an accurate summary.

Those lower rows are where the Opus 5 complaints lived. A model that rewrites the whole module to fix one line can pass every test and still lose points here.

The red box lists hard fails. Editing tests to make them pass scores zero. So does claiming a success the grading run doesn't confirm.

Then blind it. The chips at the top are random run IDs. The reviewer gets a diff and a final message with no model name attached. People reliably score the model they expect to win more kindly.

Alongside the score, write down the minutes, how often a human stepped in, and the cost.

Give the room two diffs for the same task with names removed. Have everyone score both, then compare scores before the reveal.

Now run the comparison. A configuration is the full setup: model, harness, version and settings. Pick two that differ in one place. For example, two models in the same tool at the same effort. If you change two things, you won't know which one caused the difference.

Run every task three times per configuration if you can afford it. That gives you an average pass rate and a rough pass^3, the share of tasks solved all three times.

The grid shows one result. Each column is a task. Filled squares passed. A passed 14 of 20 and B passed 10. From the error bar screen, you know those two rates overlap at this size.

The paired view gives you more. Both passed nine tasks and both failed five. Those fourteen tell you nothing about the difference. The bracket marks the six where they split. Five went to A and one to B.

Open those six transcripts. You will find out whether A is better at something you care about, or whether B tripped on one quirk of your setup.

Ask what you would conclude if all five of A's wins were refactors and B's one win was the trap task.

Your own eval

Rerun it whenever the model or the tool changes

The set pays off every time a model ships. Keep it in the repo next to the code, and review changes to it like code.

Pin versions in the results file, so a result from three months ago still says what it measured.

Go deeper: rerun on every change

New model or version→Rerun the full set before you switch your team's default.
Harness update→Rerun a five-task smoke subset. Harness changes have caused real regressions.
Using an API→Pin dated model snapshots. A "latest" alias can change underneath you.
A task's fix is now public→Replace the task once models start acing it.
Any passing run→Diff the test directory and read a sample of passing transcripts. Agents game graders.

An eval you ran once goes stale as fast as a public benchmark. The table shows when to run it again.

A new model or version means the full set, before you change your team's default. A harness update means a quick subset of about five tasks. Tool updates have caused real regressions with the model unchanged.

If you call models through an API, pin a dated snapshot. An alias like "latest" can point at a different model next month, and your old results would then describe something you no longer run.

Tasks leak too. If a fix becomes public and models start acing that task, swap it out.

The last row is about trusting your grader. Stronger agents are better at gaming tests. METR says checking for cheating is often most of the work of running its suite. The Terminal-Bench maintainers removed leaderboard entries that had hidden solutions inside the agent or pulled answers from the internet. So hide your tests, diff the test folder, and read some of the passing runs yourself.

Ask who owns the rerun on the team when a new model ships. If nobody does, the eval will be run once.

What to remember

Three things to keep

  • A score describes a model, a harness, settings, a task set, a grader and a number of attempts. Find all six before you compare.
  • Benchmarks leak, saturate and get aimed at. Cross-check a launch post with independent runs and with what specific users report.
  • Start with 10 to 20 golden tasks from your own merged PRs. Grade them blind, and rerun them when the model or the tool changes.

Go deeper: build your own eval

Keep three things from this lesson. First, a score belongs to a whole setup. That means the model, the harness, the settings, the tasks, the grader and the number of attempts. Find all six before you compare two numbers.

Second, public benchmarks leak, saturate, and get aimed at. Read launch posts next to independent evaluators, and next to specific reports from people who use the model daily.

Third, the eval that decides things for you is your own. Start with ten to twenty tasks from your merged PRs, graded blind, and run again whenever the model or the tool changes.

Homework. Each person turns two merged PRs into golden tasks, so the group has a shared set by next session.
