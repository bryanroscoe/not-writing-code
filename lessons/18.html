Work and learning

When you're learning, the code is a side effect

At work, a merged pull request that fixes the bug is a success whether you or an agent typed it.

When you're learning, what counts is the change in your head. That comes from trying, failing and working out why. If an AI does that part, you get the artifact without the learning.

At work, fixing the bug and merging the pull request is the goal, whoever types the code.

An async exercise has a different goal. You need a mental model of how the code behaves and an instinct for where bugs hide. You build those by struggling with the problem. If the agent works through it for you, you miss that practice.

The same tool that makes an experienced engineer faster can let a learner skip the work that would have made them experienced.

The evidence

Learning a library with AI cost 17 points on the quiz

In a 2026 randomized trial at Anthropic, 52 mostly junior engineers built two features with Trio, a Python async library none of them had used. Then they took a quiz.

The AI group finished about two minutes faster, which wasn't statistically significant. The biggest quiz gap was on debugging questions.

Start with the study that tested coding directly. Researchers at Anthropic took 52 engineers, mostly junior, who wrote Python every week but had never used a library called Trio. Half could use an AI assistant. Half coded by hand. Everyone built the same two features and then took a quiz.

The bars show the result. The hand-coding group averaged 67 percent. The AI group averaged 50. That is nearly two letter grades.

The AI group was about two minutes faster, and that difference could be chance. So in this study the AI group learned less and bought almost no time.

The study involved only 52 people, and the quiz came right after the task. It used a chat assistant in a sidebar. The authors expect a full agent would make the gap larger.

Scores split by how people used the AI

Some people in the AI group scored about as well as the hand-coders. They used the AI to understand the code.

The low scorers handed over the task or the debugging. These subgroups had two to seven people each, so the pattern is suggestive and unproven.

This study ran in real classrooms. Nearly a thousand high-school students in Turkey did math practice sessions in one of three ways. One group had a plain GPT-4 chat. One had GPT-4 set up as a tutor that gives hints and holds back the answer. One had no AI.

During practice, both AI groups did much better than the students with no AI.

One caution on the number. It is a relative drop in grades. The 17 points in the coding trial were percentage points on a quiz. The two numbers match by coincidence.

Watch for "so AI is bad for learning." The tutor row says the setup made the difference.

The evidence

Feeling faster doesn't tell you how you did

In a 2025 study by METR, 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer on tasks in their own repos when allowed early-2025 AI tools. Afterward they believed AI had made them about 20% faster.

The tools have improved a lot since. What has held up is that people judge their own AI-assisted work poorly.

Coursework

A course can label every assignment with an AI tier

AI use is hard to detect reliably, so instructors check understanding in other ways: oral explain-backs, code walkthroughs, live debugging of your own submission.

Course policies range from banning AI unless it's explicitly allowed to requiring it. Read each syllabus, and ask in writing before you submit when you're unsure.

Go deeper: a suggested policy

Suggested policy

Now coursework. Detecting AI use is unreliable, so a policy built on detection is weak. Instructors have better tools. They can ask you to explain your code out loud, walk through it, or debug it live.

One policy that fits those tools labels each assignment AI-free, AI-as-tutor or AI-accelerated. AI-as-tutor work lets the AI explain, hint and review, but you write what you submit. Any submission can be followed by a five-minute walkthrough.

This is a suggested policy. Yours may differ a lot. Stanford's default guidance is restrictive when a course says nothing. Harvard's CS50 allows its own AI tutor and rules out other AI tools that suggest or complete answers or code. Check the syllabus for every course.

Now careers, starting with the number people quote most. It comes from Stanford's Digital Economy Lab. The researchers use payroll records from ADP, which cover millions of American workers.

Their August 2026 revision uses data through June. Employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-exposed jobs was 19 percent below where it would have been if it had kept pace with young workers in less-exposed jobs. The gap has widened since the paper's first version in 2025.

Companies are mostly hiring fewer young people. They are not mostly laying them off. The decline is concentrated in jobs where AI substitutes for the tasks. Where AI complements workers, employment is flat or rising, especially for experienced people.

Software development is on the exposed list.

Watch for "19 percent of junior developers lost their jobs." The number is a gap against a comparison group, and it comes mostly from hires that did not happen.

The payroll data shows a gap. It does not show what caused it, and the authors say so. They call the results descriptive.

Tech over-hired during the pandemic and then corrected. Interest rates went up. Some work moved offshore. And some companies paused junior hiring while they work out what AI means for them. Several of these could be true at once.

The pattern holds when they leave out tech firms, and when they control for interest rates and remote work. That covers some of the alternatives.

The official forecast still shows growth. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects jobs for software developers, QA analysts and testers to grow 10 percent from 2025 to 2035. The average for all occupations is 3 percent.

So entry-level software work is harder to find, and AI is a plausible part of why. Nobody knows the long-run effect. If companies stop hiring juniors, they eventually run out of seniors.

Ask the room: if agents do the junior work, where do future senior engineers come from? What should companies, universities and students each do?

Generated code raises two legal questions, copyright protection and infringement. This is the United States position. Other countries differ.

The Copyright Office reported in January 2025 that existing law covers it. Output can be protected where a person determined the expressive elements. Prompts alone are unlikely to meet that bar. So what you can protect is what you authored: the parts you wrote, and how you selected, arranged and modified the output. Reviewing or approving generated code does not by itself make you its author.

Models sometimes reproduce training code. Some of it is under licenses like the GPL, which put obligations on you if you distribute it. The risk is highest for long, distinctive snippets.

There are two practical defenses. Some assistants can block or flag suggestions that match public code. License scanners in your CI catch known snippets.

Accountability

You answer for everything you ship

An agent can't be fired, sued or held to a professional standard. You can. Whatever you merge, deploy, submit or publish is yours, with its bugs, security holes and license problems.

What to remember

Three things to keep

  • While you're learning, use AI for hints and explanations, and do the work yourself.
  • Entry-level hiring is under pressure, so build and show judgment: specs, review, debugging, trade-offs.
  • You answer for what you ship and for the data you paste.

Go deeper: deskilling

While you're learning, use AI as a tutor and do the work yourself. Nobody is sure how much of the pressure on entry-level hiring comes from AI, but you can build judgment and show it. And you answer for what you ship and for the data you paste.

Using agents well takes judgment that people used to build by doing the work by hand. The likely answer is deliberate practice. Do some work with no AI for fundamentals, use AI as a tutor while you learn, and go to full acceleration once you can check the output.
