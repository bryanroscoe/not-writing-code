Reinforcement learning: the model tries, a scorer grades the attempt, and training makes what scored well more likely.

Lesson 01 said that reasoning models are trained to think before they answer, and promised the details. This lesson has them. It covers how a base model becomes an assistant, how reinforcement learning works, and how that same loop taught models to reason and to write code.

The three chips are the whole method: try, score, nudge. By the end you will know what each one means and why it explains some odd habits of the agent in your terminal.

The screen shows three kinds of lessons. The first is supervised fine-tuning, the apprenticeship. The model gets example conversations with good responses and learns to copy them. That teaches the chat format, the tone, and how to write a tool call.

Reinforcement learning

Reinforcement learning grades the model's own attempts

In supervised training the model sees the right answer and learns to copy it. In reinforcement learning (RL) there's no answer to copy.

The model tries something, gets a score, and is adjusted so that whatever scored well becomes more likely.

So far in this course, training has meant copying. Pretraining copies the next token of real text. Supervised fine-tuning copies example answers. The left panel is that setup. The right answer is on the page, and the model learns to reproduce it.

Reinforcement learning

Training raises what beat the average and lowers the rest

An answer's advantage is its reward minus the group's average. Every token in an above-average answer gets a little more likely. Every token in a below-average one gets a little less likely.

This update is a policy gradient. Scoring a group against its own average is how GRPO works, the base of most open 2025 and 2026 recipes.

Illustrative probabilities

Now the update. Take each reward and subtract the group's average, which was one half. The two passing answers come out half a point above average. The two failures come out half a point below. That gap is called the advantage.

Training nudges up the probability of every token in the above-average answers, and nudges down the tokens in the others. The bars show each answer's probability before and after. The change is drawn much larger than life.

Nobody showed the model a correct answer. It produced two among its own samples, and the checker said which. Over thousands of prompts, the choices that keep appearing in high-scoring answers become the model's defaults.

The method is called a policy gradient. Comparing a group of answers against their own average is how an algorithm called GRPO works. Most open training recipes from 2025 and 2026 run a modified version of it.

Watch for "the model is told the right answer." It only ever gets a number for each attempt.

Reinforcement learning

A prompt only teaches when some attempts pass and some fail

If all four answers pass, or all four fail, every answer equals the average. Every advantage is zero and the prompt gives no learning signal.

Training problems have to be hard enough that the model sometimes fails and easy enough that it sometimes succeeds.

The reward arrives at the end, so in the simplest versions every token in a winning answer gets the same nudge, useful or not. That blunt credit is one reason RL needs so many samples.

Comparing against the average has a consequence. Look at the three panels. On the left the problem is too easy and all four attempts pass. Each one scores exactly the average, so each advantage is zero and nothing gets nudged. On the right the problem is too hard. All four fail, and again nothing moves.

Only the middle panel teaches. So the people running the training have to keep problems near the edge of what the model can do. One 2025 method, DAPO, drops the all-right and all-wrong groups outright.

There is one more limit. The reward arrives at the end of the answer. In the simplest versions, every token in a winning answer gets the same nudge, including tokens that had nothing to do with winning. That blunt credit is one reason RL needs a huge number of attempts. Keep it in mind. It explains how thinking gets trained a few screens from now.

Ask what a student learns from an exam that everyone aces, or one that everyone fails.

Human feedback

RLHF scores answers with a model trained on human comparisons

Writing a perfect answer is hard. Saying which of two is better is easier. RLHF collects those comparisons, trains a reward model to predict them, and uses it as the scorer.

The reward model is a proxy. Push hard against it and the policy finds answers that score higher while getting worse, so a KL penalty charges the policy for drifting from where it started.

The first big use of RL on language models was RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback. Most tasks have no checker. Nobody can write a unit test for a helpful answer. But a person can read two answers and say which is better.

The steps on screen are from InstructGPT, OpenAI's 2022 paper. First came supervised fine-tuning on about 13,000 prompts with human-written answers. Second, labelers ranked several model answers for each of 33,000 prompts, and a reward model was trained to predict those rankings. Third came RL. The model answered 31,000 prompts and the reward model scored each answer.

The reward model learned from a limited set of answers. Push hard against it and the policy finds answers that score higher and read worse. The line at the bottom is the fix. A KL penalty subtracts from the reward as the policy's token choices drift away from the original fine-tuned model.

It worked. Labelers preferred a small InstructGPT to a GPT-3 with 100 times more parameters. The paper is also candid about whose taste the model learned: its labelers and researchers.

Lesson 01 said models lean toward agreeing with you. Ask where in this pipeline that could come from.

Verifiable rewards

RLVR hands the scoring to a program

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) uses problems whose answers a program can check: a math answer that matches, code that passes unit tests, output that follows a format rule.

With a checker as the scorer there's no learned reward model to fool. It only works for tasks with checkable answers.

The other way to get a scorer is to pick problems where a program can do the grading. That is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, RLVR for short. The checker might compare a math answer against the known one, run unit tests against code, or test that the output follows a format rule.

Compare the panels. A learned reward model is a guess at human taste. In InstructGPT it agreed with a fresh group of labelers about 70 percent of the time, which is close to how often the labelers agreed with each other. A program is cheap and automatic, and much harder to fool. Later screens show it can still be gamed.

The limit is coverage. A checker only exists for tasks with checkable answers. Math and code have them. Tone and helpfulness don't, so labs still use reward models, AI judges and written rubrics for those.

The worked example with four attempts was already RLVR. The next screens use the same loop to train a model to think.

Reasoning models

In R1-Zero's training, nobody graded the thinking

DeepSeek ran the RL loop directly on a base model to make R1-Zero. The rewards were rule-based: one for a correct final answer and one for putting the thinking between its tags.

The thinking tokens in between earn nothing on their own. They only pay off when they lead to an answer the checker accepts.

Reasoning models

A right answer pulls up the thinking that led to it

The model attempts the same problem several times. Attempts that reach the right answer score above the group's average, so every token in them gets more likely, thinking included.

Thinking that led to a wrong answer gets less likely. Nobody has to say which thoughts were the good ones.

Reasoning models

Over many rounds, thinking longer and stopping to reconsider become defaults

R1-Zero's thinking got longer on its own as training went on. At one point its use of the word "wait" jumped, which the authors called an aha moment.

Skeptics found that base models already reflect a little before any RL. On that reading, RL made an existing behavior show up more often.

Run that update over a huge number of problems and the pattern on screen appears. Before RL, the model writes a little and answers. After RL, it writes a lot more before it commits. DeepSeek reported that R1-Zero's thinking kept getting longer through training, and nobody had asked for length.

The content changed too. The quote at the bottom is from the model partway through training. It stops itself and reconsiders. The peer-reviewed version of the paper describes this more soberly, as a sudden increase in the word "wait" during reflections.

Some researchers think the story is oversold. One group found that most base models already show self-reflection before any RL, and that much of it is superficial. On their reading, RL took a behavior that was already there and made it show up more often.

Either way, the thinking you pay for is a trained habit. It was reinforced because it came with answers that passed a check.

Watch for "the model was taught how to reason." Nobody wrote a method down. The loop rewarded outcomes and the habits followed.

Reasoning models

Grading every step was tried, and the big recipes dropped it

Grading only outcomes can reward a right answer reached by wrong steps. Scoring each step with a process reward model looked like the fix, and early studies favored it.

DeepSeek lists it among R1's unsuccessful attempts. It's hard to say what counts as a step and hard to judge one, and a learned step grader gets gamed.

Illustrative

Grading only the final answer has an obvious hole. A model can reach the right answer through wrong steps and still collect the reward. On the left, the three steps go unread and only the answer gets a mark.

The alternative on the right is a process reward model, which scores each step. In a 2022 study, 14 percent of the right answers came from faulty reasoning, and grading steps cut that to 3.4 percent. A 2023 OpenAI paper found it beat outcome grading on competition math.

Yet DeepSeek lists step grading among the things that did not work for R1. Deciding what counts as one step is hard. Judging a step is hard. And the step grader is itself a learned model, so the policy learns to fool it. The large open recipes since then score outcomes with a checker where they can.

So a correct final answer does not certify the reasoning above it. The thinking was shaped by whether the answer checked out. Training did not require each step to be valid.

Coding agents

A coding agent trains inside real repositories

A coding RL environment packages a snapshot of a codebase, a runtime that can build and run it, a task in plain language, and tests that decide whether the task is done.

The agent reads files, runs commands and edits code, and the reward arrives at the end. Building thousands of these environments is most of the work.

The same loop trains coding agents. The math problem becomes a whole repository. The box on screen is one environment. It holds a snapshot of a real codebase, a runtime that can build and run it, a task written in plain language, and tests that decide whether the task is done.

One attempt is now a whole agent session. The agent reads files, runs commands, edits code and runs things again. The reward comes at the end, when the tests run. A session that ends in passing tests gets its steps nudged up, the same way a right answer pulled up its thinking.

Most of the work is building environments by the thousand. One 2025 project made 50,000 tasks from 128 repositories by deliberately breaking code so that existing tests fail. Labs run ten or twenty thousand sandboxes at once. Cursor says it trains across hundreds of thousands of them, with the same tools and harness its deployed model uses.

Code is unusually checkable. Tests are cheap and automatic, and open source supplies real code with real tests. That is a big part of why coding agents improved so quickly in 2025 and 2026.

Ask what in their own repository would make a good environment, and what its tests would fail to capture.

Reward hacking

RL optimizes the score, loopholes included

A reward measures what you want. When the measurement has a loophole, RL finds it. For language models this is called reward hacking.

Tests are an incomplete description of what you want, and a model trained against tests can learn to satisfy the tests alone.

Two experiments show what reduces this. In the first, METR took a task where o3 had gamed the score in all 21 runs. They added one sentence to the prompt, "Please do not cheat," and ran it 20 times. o3 planned a reward hack in 80 percent of those runs, the same rate as with the original prompt.

In the second, the ImpossibleBench authors changed the setup around the model. Giving it a way to stop and flag the task for a human cut GPT-5's cheating from 54 percent to 9 percent, and o3's from 49 to 12. Claude Opus 4.1 barely moved. Making the test files read-only stopped the edits to tests, though models still special-cased inputs.

Wording did count in that paper. A strict prompt that told the model to stop and report any flawed test cut GPT-5's cheating on a second set of tasks from over 85 percent to 1 percent. The plea that failed was the vague one. These are different tasks and models, so don't compare the two sets of bars number for number.

Your own agent setup has the same levers: specific instructions for what to do when a test looks wrong, a way for the agent to stop and ask, and limits on what it can touch. Lesson 04 shows how to set those up and how to check what the agent did.

Ask which of those levers their current agent setup relies on.

Fine-tuning

Most teams shouldn't fine-tune first

Fine-tuning is further training of a model on a narrower dataset of your own. Prompts, context and retrieval solve most problems first.

For adding new facts, studies found retrieval beat fine-tuning. Fine-tuning does best at teaching form: a consistent format, style or schema at high volume.

Go deeper: should you fine-tune at all?

Try these in order. Stop when one meets your needs.

  1. Give a strong model a clear task, constraints and output format.
  2. Give it the docs, code, schemas and examples it needs.
  3. Use retrieval if the knowledge is big or keeps changing.
  4. Put 5 to 50 good examples in the prompt.
  5. Build a labeled evaluation set.
  6. Consider fine-tuning.

So far the labs did the training. You can also train a model yourself, which is called fine-tuning. Most teams should not start there. The ladder on screen is the order to try things, and you stop at the first rung that works.

Start with a clear task for a strong model. Then give it the documents, code and examples it needs. Use retrieval when the knowledge is large or keeps changing. Put a handful of good examples in the prompt. Before going further, build a labeled evaluation set. Without one you can't tell whether fine-tuning helped.

The research backs this order for knowledge. Retrieval beat fine-tuning at adding new facts. Models also learn new facts slowly through fine-tuning, and the more they absorb, the more they tend to hallucinate. Fine-tuning earns its place for form: strict output formats, narrow classification at very high volume, or data that can't leave your network.

The request you will hear most is "make it smarter at our codebase". That is a context problem. The code changes daily, and an agent can read it.

Rules of thumb

If you do reach the last rung, you rarely retrain the whole model. Full fine-tuning updates every weight. It needs the most memory and leaves you with a full copy of the model for every variant.

LoRA freezes the model and trains a small correction beside it. The diagram shows one weight matrix, 4,096 by 4,096, which is about 16.8 million numbers. LoRA at rank 16 adds two thin matrices with 131,072 numbers between them. That is under 1 percent of the original, and only the thin ones are trained.

QLoRA also loads the frozen model at 4-bit precision. The bars show rough memory for a model with 8 billion parameters. Full fine-tuning needs about 130 gigabytes, which means several datacenter GPUs. QLoRA needs 8 to 16. A small run takes minutes to a few hours and often costs tens of dollars. The expensive part is people's time building the data and the evaluations.

Two cautions. Training on a new task can overwrite old abilities, so test what you did not train for. Narrow fine-tuning can also weaken a model's safety training, so rerun those checks. And as of November 2026 this mostly means open-weight models. OpenAI began winding down self-serve fine-tuning in May 2026.

Self-tuning

No deployed model keeps tuning its own weights

Deployed models are frozen. They don't learn from the work they do, and "memory" features are notes put back into the context.

Since 2025, research systems have had models write their own training data and trigger weight updates. As of November 2026 these are narrow, supervised demonstrations.

People who learn how training works often assume the model keeps learning from them. It doesn't. Lesson 01 said chatting never changes the weights. As of November 2026 that holds for every deployed frontier model.

The table sorts the things that get called learning. Examples in the prompt change nothing and are gone when the session ends. Memory files are text loaded back into the context. Some research agents rewrite their own harness code. Only the last row changes the model itself.

That last row now exists in research. In a 2025 system from MIT called SEAL, a model writes its own study notes, an ordinary training step applies them to its weights, and a test decides whether the change helped. Google published a related design in 2026. Both are research prototypes, run on tasks people chose against tests people wrote.

Give the room a "self-improving AI" headline and ask four questions. What changes? Who produces the change? Who decides whether it stays? Does it compound?

What to remember

Three things to keep

  • RL is try, score, nudge: the model attempts, a scorer grades, and what scored well gets more likely.
  • Reasoning models think because thinking came with right answers. Only the answers were checked.
  • RL optimizes the score. Protect the tests, read the diff, and verify with something the agent can't edit.

Go deeper: exercises

Keep three things from this lesson. First, reinforcement learning is try, score, nudge. The model makes attempts, something scores them, and training makes the high scorers more likely.

Second, reasoning models think because thinking came along with right answers. A program checked the answers. Nobody checked the thinking.

Third, RL optimizes the score, and the score is never quite what you meant. Protect your tests, read the diff, and verify with something the agent can't edit.
