This lesson is about living with the pace of this field. New models, tool versions and benchmark revisions arrive almost every week, and quality goes down as well as up.

By the end you will have a way to diagnose a bad week, a way to read a launch post, and a routine that takes about thirty minutes a week.

You can't track all of it, and you don't need to.

This is one three-month stretch of 2026. In late July, Anthropic released Claude Opus 5. Six days later Terminal-Bench 3.0 came out, and four weeks after that version 4.0 replaced it. September brought Claude Fable 5.1, then GPT-6 Astra two days later. On September 22, Opus 5.5 and two more GPT-6 models landed on the same day. Google's Gemini 4 Argon followed on October 13.

The tools around the models move just as fast. OpenAI's Codex command line tool reached version 0.170 on November 18. Twelve days earlier it was at 0.167. So the scoreboards change along with the models they score. Nobody reads all of this. The rest of the lesson is about deciding what deserves your attention.

Ask the room how many of these releases they tried, and how many changed how they work.

Six skills are on screen, and each has a reason it outlives any single model. Every model is limited by what you show it, so giving it the right context stays useful. Models are still confidently wrong some of the time, so you keep verifying. Attacks go after the agent pattern itself, so the security habits carry over.

Reliability drops as tasks get longer, for every model. That keeps small, checkable steps worth the effort. Your own eval set tells you what a release means for your work. Prices and limits will keep changing, and the habit of measuring them stays.

The box at the bottom is a quick test for any new technique. Ask whether it will still be useful after the next model ships. Writing a clear spec and a test first passes. A magic phrase that makes one model behave usually fails.

The advice from 2023 and 2024 bears this out. Prompt tricks mostly aged badly. Giving the model examples, context and a way to check its work aged well.

Ask for one piece of 2023 prompting advice people remember. Then ask whether it still works.

Ups and downs

The quality you feel comes from a stack of five layers

Any layer can change without notice, and quality goes down as well as up.

People usually blame the weights. In the documented incidents, the cause was more often in the middle of the stack.

When a tool feels worse, something changed, and there are five places it could be. Read the stack from the top. First there is you: the task, the prompt, how long the session has run, how big the repo is.

Next is the harness, the program around the model. It has a version, a system prompt, a default effort level, a set of tools, and compaction. Compaction is the harness summarizing older parts of a long session to make room.

Under that is the product: your plan, your usage limits, which model your request gets routed to. Then serving, which is the hardware and software that run the model. The weights are at the bottom.

The usual complaint is that the company nerfed the model, which points at the bottom layer. The next screen goes through the incidents the companies have confirmed. More of them started in the three middle layers.

Ask where "they nerfed the model" sits on this stack, and what evidence it would take to place a problem there.

Ups and downs

Users noticed before the labs' own evals did

The postmortems name the same obstacles. Evals are noisy, changes overlap, and rollouts are staged, so you and a coworker can be on different versions the same day.

You can't verify a vendor's statement about its own quality. You can rerun your golden tasks: real, solved pieces of your own work, frozen so you can replay them.

The labs' own monitoring often missed what users were reporting. The first two quotes are from Anthropic's postmortems. In 2025 it wrote that its evaluations didn't capture the degradation. In 2026 it wrote that the problems were hard to tell apart from normal variation in feedback.

The chips at the bottom list why. Privacy controls limit how engineers can look at real sessions. Evals are noisy, and Anthropic found one test showing a 3 percent drop only after weeks of reports. Several changes can overlap and reach different users. Staged rollouts mean two people can run different versions on the same day.

Anthropic and OpenAI both responded by having more of their own staff use the builds the public gets. That tells you their internal users had been seeing a different product.

The third quote is a claim you have to take on trust. Anthropic says it never reduces quality because of demand or load. You can't test that from outside. You can rerun your own golden tasks, which is why they anchor the rest of this lesson.

Ask how an outside user could test a claim like the third quote. There is no clean answer.

This is the order to check things when a tool feels worse. Start at the top and stop at the first box that applies.

First, look at the vendor's status page. If there is an incident, wait it out or use another tool for the day. Second, look at what changed in your tool. It may have updated itself, or its default model, effort level or context setting may have moved. If so, pin or roll back, and rerun a small smoke test.

Third, run your golden tasks. If they fail more than usual, you probably have a real regression. Collect specifics and report it. A report like "since this version it ignores my test command" is worth far more than "it got dumber".

Fourth, if the golden tasks still pass, look at today's task. A bigger repo, a longer session or a vaguer spec makes the same model look worse. Start a fresh session with a smaller step.

If none of that explains it, write a line in your vibes log and check again in a week.

Ask someone to describe their last "it got worse" moment, and walk it through the four checks as a group.

Pinning

Pin what you can, so upgrades happen on your schedule

Apps and subscription tools change system prompts, default effort, routing and context handling without changing the model's name.

Pinning turns some of those surprises into upgrades you choose.

Go deeper: silent changes and how to pin things

A model behind an API→Use a dated snapshot for anything automated. Upgrade deliberately.
Your CLI→Pin the version and turn off auto-update. Upgrade after a smoke test.
Your own configuration→Keep AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md and MCP settings in version control, so you can tell what you changed.
An app's system prompt→You can't pin it. Anthropic publishes system prompt release notes for its apps. Read them when behavior shifts.

A chat app or a subscription tool can change a lot while the model keeps the same name. The system prompt, the default effort, the routing and the way context gets handled can all move without an announcement.

You can't stop that, but you can cut down on the surprises. Four things are on screen. APIs usually offer dated snapshots, which are fixed versions of a model. Use those for anything automated, and move to a newer one on purpose.

Command line tools let you pin a version and switch off auto-update. Then you upgrade when you have time to run a quick smoke test afterward.

Keep your own configuration in version control. That covers your instruction files and your tool server settings. When quality drops, the history shows what you changed.

The last row is the one you can't pin. An app's system prompt belongs to the vendor. Anthropic publishes release notes for the system prompts in its apps, so read those when behavior shifts.

Ask who knows which version of their CLI they are running right now, and whether auto-update is on.

Switching

Switch tools only on strong evidence

Switching costs you new habits, new configuration and new failure modes.

A drop on your golden tasks that repeats a week later is evidence. One bad afternoon is not.

Go deeper: when to switch tools

Good reasons

  • Your golden tasks show a consistent drop across two or more runs, a week apart.
  • A competitor beats your tool on your golden tasks by more than your error bar, at a cost you can afford.
  • Pricing, limits, data policy or security posture changed in a way that affects you.
  • A long bad stretch with no fix, no postmortem and no communication.

Bad reasons

  • One bad afternoon
  • A launch-day benchmark chart
  • A viral thread
  • Fear of missing out

Changing your main tool is expensive. You have to build new habits, redo your configuration, and learn a new set of failure modes. So the bar for switching should be high.

The left panel lists reasons that clear it. Your golden tasks show a consistent drop, on at least two runs a week apart. Or a competitor beats your current tool on those same tasks, by more than the noise in your measurements, at a price you can pay.

The other two are about the vendor. Pricing, limits, data policy or security may change in a way that affects you. Or the vendor may sit in a long bad stretch and say nothing about it.

The right panel lists the reasons people switch anyway. One bad afternoon, a launch-day chart, a viral thread, and fear of missing out. None of them is a measurement of your own work.

Ask who has switched main tools in the last six months, and which panel their reason belongs in.

Sources

A rumor gains credibility each time it's repeated

Rumor laundering starts with an anonymous post. A blog picks it up, an "AI news" article adds invented specifics, and soon the claim is described as "reported".

When a claim affects a decision, trace it to a primary source or treat it as unverified.

Before choosing sources, it helps to see how bad information travels. The chain on screen is called rumor laundering.

It starts with an anonymous post. A blog repeats it and adds "sources say". An auto-generated news site picks that up and fills in details nobody supplied, like a context size and a launch day. After that, people describe the claim as reported.

Each step looks more official than the last. The box at the bottom shows what stayed the same. The evidence was one anonymous post the whole way through.

Launch days make this worse. They are built for attention, with leaks, countdowns, charts and viral demos. Benchmark tables come out before independent tests. Creators get rewarded for saying everything has changed. None of that is evidence about your work.

So when a claim affects a decision, follow it back to a primary source. If you can't find one, treat the claim as unverified.

Every week, spend thirty minutes. Ten go to the changelogs of the two or three tools you use. Fifteen go to one or two newsletters. In the last five, add at most one item to a try list. That cap keeps the list from turning into a backlog.

Illustrative

One person can't follow everything, and on a team nobody has to. A radar is a shared file that records what the team uses and why. The format comes from the Thoughtworks Technology Radar.

It has four rings. Adopt means proven on your golden tasks and recommended as the default. Trial means a few people are using it on real work. Assess means someone is giving it an afternoon. Hold means don't start new work with it, and the entry says why.

The file on screen is an example. Each row has the evidence, an owner, and a date to look again. A radar can hold practices as well as products. In the Hold row, auto-merging agent pull requests caused two incidents, so review stays mandatory.

Running it is light. Each month one person is the scout. They skim the sources, propose items, and run an afternoon evaluation on anything promising. The team meets for thirty minutes, and an item moves at most one ring per meeting, with evidence.

For leads, the file doubles as the record of why the team uses what it uses. That helps with budget conversations and onboarding.

Draft a radar live. List the tools the room uses, place each in a ring, and write down what evidence is missing.

When something on your try list looks promising, this is how to evaluate it in one afternoon. The bar across the top shows where the four hours go. Almost half is spent running your own tasks.

Spend the first twenty minutes reading. Go through the launch post, pricing, limits and the system card's limitations. Write down two or three specific claims to test, like "better at long refactors". Spend the next twenty on independent signals. If nobody independent has published yet, note that and trust the claims less.

Then run ten golden tasks in the harness you use, with your normal settings. Hide which model produced which output before you grade.

After that, give it one real task from this week's backlog and watch how it works. See whether it stays in scope and asks good questions. Then compare tokens, quota and speed with your current default.

Use the last fifteen minutes to decide. Write three sentences with the evidence and a date to look again. If it's close, wait two weeks.

Homework. Run a shortened version with five golden tasks on the most recent release that affects you, and bring the three sentences back.

What to remember

Three things to keep

  • Releases arrive weekly, and the skills that make you effective change slowly.
  • When quality drops, check the status page, your own setup and your golden tasks before you blame the model.
  • Trust results on your own tasks over launch posts, wait two weeks, and keep a second tool warm.

Go deeper: how durable is your knowledge

Keep three things from this lesson. First, releases arrive every week, and you can let most of them pass. Context, verification, security and review change slowly.

Second, when a tool feels worse, check in order. Look at the status page, then at what changed in your own setup, then at your golden tasks. In the documented record, the cause was more often above the weights.

Third, your own tasks are the evidence that counts. Launch posts are marketing, one bad release doesn't mean a lab has lost its way, and one great chart doesn't make a model better to work with. Wait two weeks, test, and keep a second tool ready.

Have each person set up a vibes log before they leave: date, model, harness and version, task type, what happened.
