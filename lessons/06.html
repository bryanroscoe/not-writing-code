Lesson 05 sorted models into closed weight and open weight. Some you can download, and others you can only call over an API. This lesson covers that split. By the end you'll be able to read a model license, estimate whether a model fits on your machine, and say where your code goes when you use one.

Weights

Open weight means you can download the model

Weights are the numbers training adjusted, billions or trillions of them. Closed-weight labs keep them on their servers and sell access. Open-weight labs publish them as files.

With the files you can run the model on hardware you control, fine-tune it and inspect its internals. You still don't know what data it was trained on or how to rebuild it.

Weights are the numbers inside a model, the ones training adjusted. A model has billions of them, and the largest have trillions. Whoever holds those numbers can run the model.

A closed-weight lab keeps them on its own servers. You send a prompt to its API or app and get text back. Claude, GPT-6 and Gemini work this way. An open-weight lab publishes the weights as files you can download. Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4 and Gemma 4 are examples as of November 2026.

With the files you can run the model on hardware you control, change it by fine-tuning, and look at its internals. You still can't see what data it was trained on, and you can't rebuild it from scratch.

Ask who has run a model on their own machine, and which one.

Weight files

Weights are big files of numbers, and one old format can run code

Safetensors is the standard format on Hugging Face. GGUF is the single-file format that llama.cpp, Ollama and LM Studio use.

Older PyTorch files are built on Python's pickle and can run arbitrary code when loaded. Treat them like executables from strangers.

On disk, weights are large files of numbers. You'll meet three kinds.

Safetensors is the standard format on Hugging Face, the main site for sharing models. A safetensors file holds arrays of numbers and no executable code. GGUF is a single-file format used by llama.cpp, Ollama and LM Studio. A GGUF file usually comes already compressed to fewer bits per weight.

The third kind is the old PyTorch format, with file names ending in dot bin or dot P T. Those are built on Python's pickle, and loading one can run arbitrary code on your machine. Treat it like a program a stranger sent you.

One caveat. Safetensors protects you from the weight file. The code shipped next to it and the software that loads it can still be unsafe.

Open a model page on Hugging Face and find the file list. Have the room name each format.

Openness

Open weight isn't open source

The Open Source Initiative's definition for AI (October 2024) asks for the weights, the complete code used to train and run the system, and enough detail about the training data to build an equivalent. All of it has to be usable for any purpose.

Most "open" models publish only the weights.

People say open source when they mean open weight. The two are different. The Open Source Initiative published a definition for AI in October 2024. To qualify, a release needs the weights, the complete code used to train and run the system, and enough detail about the training data that a skilled person could build an equivalent. Licenses

Eight things to check before you build on an open model

Many companies keep an approved-model list. Get the model on it before you build.

This is engineering guidance. Legal advice comes from your legal team.

Go deeper: the license checklist

  1. Commercial use, or non-commercial by default
  2. Thresholds on users, revenue or hosting, for you and your customers
  3. "Built with X" display and naming rules
  4. An acceptable-use policy that can change later
  5. Rights withheld in your region
  6. Training other models on outputs, and what fine-tunes inherit
  7. Patents and warranty
  8. Your legal team's approved-model list

Of the eight checks, the first three catch most problems. Check whether commercial use is allowed at all. Look for thresholds on users, revenue or hosting, and think about your customers as well as your own company. Check for display rules, such as showing "Built with" and the model's name.

The next ones are easier to miss. An acceptable-use policy can be pulled in by reference and changed later. Some rights are withheld by region. Llama 4's policy withholds its multimodal models from people and companies based in the EU. Check whether you can train other models on the outputs, and whether a fine-tune inherits the license. On patents, Apache 2.0 includes a grant and MIT doesn't mention them.

Last, many companies keep an approved-model list. Get the model on it first. This is engineering guidance, and legal advice comes from your legal team.

Why labs release

Labs give weights away for strategic reasons

A frontier model costs at least tens of millions of dollars to train. Releasing it buys adoption, hiring, pricing pressure on rivals and customers for the lab's own hosted API.

The strategy can change. In 2026 Meta kept its best Muse models closed and released two open ones.

The safety debate

A weight release can't be undone

Open weights let defenders and researchers work without asking permission. Once a model is out, anyone can strip its safeguards, and nobody can recall it.

Most current policy treats today's open models as acceptable and monitors the frontier.

Go deeper: the safety debate

For release

  • Defenders need no permission. In July 2026 Hugging Face's team analyzed an attack with a self-hosted open model after commercial APIs blocked their requests.
  • Data can stay on your machines.
  • Researchers can study the internals.
  • No single point of control.

Against release

  • Safeguards come off cheaply. A copy of Muse Spark Open with its refusals removed was on Hugging Face two days after its October 2026 release.
  • Dangerous capability spreads with a lag. Epoch estimated 6 to 7 months on cyber tasks in October 2026.
  • Agent security varies. NIST's CAISI found agents on DeepSeek R1-0528 were 12 times more likely than US frontier models to follow hijacking instructions (2025).

People who study this disagree about whether releasing weights is safe. The two panels give each side's strongest points.

For release: defenders and researchers can use the model without asking anyone. In July 2026, Hugging Face's team was analyzing an attack by autonomous agents. Commercial model APIs blocked their requests, so they used a self-hosted open model. Open weights also let researchers study a model's internals, and they keep a widely used technology out of one company's control.

Against release: it is permanent, and the safety training can be removed. Research showed that cheap fine-tuning strips it, and that refusals can be edited out of the weights directly. That edit is called abliteration. A copy of Muse Spark Open with its refusals removed was on Hugging Face two days after release.

Capability also spreads. In October 2026 Epoch estimated that open models trail the closed frontier by 6 to 7 months on cyber tasks. Most policy today accepts current open models and watches the frontier.

Quantization stores each weight with fewer bits. 8-bit is usually close to lossless. 4-bit is the common local default, with a modest loss that shows up in coding and long reasoning before casual chat.

Context takes memory on top of the weights

While generating, the model stores keys and values for every token in the context. This KV cache grows with context length.

The weights aren't the whole bill. While it generates, the model keeps keys and values for every token in the context, so it doesn't have to recompute them. This is the KV cache, and it grows with every token you add.

The formula on screen gives the size per token. For an illustrative model with 48 layers, it comes to about a fifth of a megabyte per token. A 32,000-token context then needs about 6 gigabytes on top of the weights. The bars carry the same arithmetic to other lengths. At 128,000 tokens the cache is bigger than the 4-bit weights of a 30 billion parameter model.

Many 2026 models use attention variants that shrink the cache a lot, so read the model card before you trust this estimate. Then budget for the context length you want, the number of requests running at once, and the runtime's own buffers.

Watch for "it fits, the file is only 17 gigabytes." Ask what context length they plan to run.

Hardware math

Memory follows total parameters, speed follows active ones

A mixture-of-experts (MoE) model has many expert sub-networks and uses only a few for each token. Fast inference usually keeps all of them in memory.

Almost every frontier open model of 2026 is built this way, with roughly 2 to 6% of its parameters active per token.

Most large open models are mixtures of experts. The network holds many expert sub-networks, and each token goes through only a few of them. The grid on screen shows the idea, with two cells lit out of fifty.

That gives a model two sizes. Total parameters set how much memory the weights need, because fast inference keeps every expert loaded. Active parameters are the ones a token uses, and they help estimate the computation per token.

Gemma 4's 26B-A4B model stores 26 billion parameters and uses about 4 billion per token. It needs the memory of a 26 billion parameter model. Kimi K3 stores 2.8 trillion and uses 104 billion. Its 4-bit weights are about 1.56 terabytes, which is data-center hardware.

Fewer active parameters mean less computation. They don't promise the speed of a dense model that small, since routing and memory access cost time too.

Ask the room: a model card says 816B-A42B. Roughly how much memory do its 4-bit weights need? Answer: size it on the 816 billion, so around 450 to 500 gigabytes.

Memory bandwidth caps tokens per second

Generating a token means reading all the active weights from memory once. Bandwidth divided by the bytes of active weights gives a rough ceiling, and real output is slower.

An MoE model reads far fewer bytes per token, which is why MoE models are popular locally.

Speed has its own rule of thumb. To produce one token, the machine reads every active weight from memory once. So the speed of your memory sets a ceiling.

Divide memory bandwidth by the bytes of active weights. Take a dense 30 billion parameter model at 4-bit, about 17 gigabytes, on a machine that moves about 270 gigabytes a second. 270 divided by 17 is about 16 tokens per second. That's the ceiling, and real output is slower.

Now take a mixture-of-experts model with 3 billion active parameters. Each token reads about 1.7 gigabytes. On the same machine it can run several times faster. That's a big part of why these models are popular for local use.

Hardware math

Frontier open models don't fit on a laptop

A discrete GPU needs the whole model in its own VRAM to run fast. An Apple silicon Mac shares one pool of unified memory between CPU and GPU, so a 64 to 128 GB Mac loads models no consumer GPU can, at lower bandwidth.

The headline open models need a server rack. "Local" usually means a model one or two tiers smaller.

One command runs a model on your laptop

Ollama is the easiest start, and LM Studio is the desktop-app route. Each runs a local server your tools can call. vLLM and SGLang are GPU servers for a team or a product.

Privacy

Your data goes where the model runs

Self-hosting a Chinese lab's weights keeps your prompts and code on your own machines. Calling that lab's hosted API sends them to its servers.

Those are separate risk decisions. Check the hosting region and terms of the service you call.

Self-hosting

Self-hosting fixes data flow and adds new risks

Model behavior travels with the weights. An open model running an agent with shell access is as exposed to prompt injection as a closed one, sometimes more.

Download weights only from the publisher's official repository, and check hashes.

Model bias and prompt-injection susceptibility stay the same. CAISI found DeepSeek models repeated inaccurate Chinese Communist Party narratives four times as often as US reference models (2025).

Self-hosting removes one risk, which is your data leaving. It also gives you a frozen model. A closed API can be updated or retired, and a file on your disk stays the same. That helps in regulated work.

It adds the risks in the right panel. You now run an inference server that needs patching. A local server can end up listening on every network interface with no authentication. Model files from unofficial mirrors may not be what the publisher released. And no vendor is watching for abuse.

Some things don't change at all. A model's behavior travels with its weights. For the hardest agentic coding and large refactors, start with the closed frontier models, and compare them against current open models in your own harness. If code or data can't leave your network, self-host an open model. For bulk jobs like tagging and extraction at millions of calls, compare a hosted open model with the cheapest closed tier on price per token.

Open is also the default when you need to fine-tune on your own data, run offline, or keep a frozen model for an audit. A small team with nobody to run servers should lean closed.

What to remember

Three things to keep

  • Open weight means downloadable weights under some license. Re-read the license at every upgrade.
  • Memory is parameters times bytes per parameter, plus room for context. A laptop runs a 30B model, and frontier open models need a rack.
  • Your data goes where the model runs. Self-hosting keeps it on your machines, and a hosted API sends it to that provider.

Go deeper: when to choose open vs closed

Keep three things. First, open weight means you can download the weights under some license. That license may be far from open source, and it can change with the next version.

Second, the memory a model needs is its parameter count times the bytes per parameter, plus room for context. That puts a 30 billion parameter model on a good laptop and the frontier open models in a data center.

Third, your data goes where the computation runs. Self-hosting keeps it on your machines. A hosted API sends it to that provider, whoever trained the weights.
