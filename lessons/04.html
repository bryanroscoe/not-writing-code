Vocabulary

Alignment, safeguards and security are three separate jobs

People say "safety" for all three. Labs keep them apart. Anthropic's safety roadmap tracks security, alignment, safeguards and policy as separate areas.

This lesson covers the model itself. Securing an app you build on a model, prompt injection included, is Lesson 14.

Safeguards are about people misusing the model. A classifier that blocks bioweapon instructions is a safeguard. Security is about theft and tampering. One pair gets mixed up a lot. A jailbreak tries to get the model to break its maker's rules. That's a safety problem, and it belongs in this lesson. Prompt injection tricks an application into obeying text from a stranger. That's a security problem, and Lesson 14 covers it.

Ask the room which of the three jobs a prompt injection attack falls under. Answer: security, because the target is the application.

A second way to sort things is by who wanted the harm. With misuse, a person wanted it. The model may be doing exactly what it was trained to do. The alignment problem

You can't hand a model your intentions

You hand it a training signal: a reward, a loss, a set of tests. The alignment problem is the gap between that signal and what you meant.

Specification gaming is that gap where you can see it: "a behaviour that satisfies the literal specification of an objective without achieving the intended outcome" (DeepMind, 2020).

Go deeper: the alignment problem

CoastRunners, a boat-racing game (OpenAI, 2016). The agent learned to circle the same targets and never finished the race.

Illustrative sketch

You can't give a model what you want directly. You give it a signal: a reward, a loss number, a set of tests. Training pushes the model toward the signal. Anything the signal fails to capture is left open.

The sketch is the boat from lesson 03. In OpenAI's 2016 experiment with a racing game called CoastRunners, the agent earned points for hitting targets along the course. It learned to circle one cluster of targets and never finish the race. The score kept going up.

Researchers call this specification gaming. The agent meets the literal objective and misses the intended outcome. Lesson 03 used the usual name for it in language models, reward hacking. A coding model that special-cases your tests is doing what the boat did.

Ask for a human example of gaming a metric. Lines of code and closed-ticket counts usually come up.

The alignment problem

The objective can be wrong, or the model can learn a different one

Outer alignment asks whether the objective is what you want. Inner alignment asks whether the model learned that objective or something else that scored well in training.

Think of a student. The outer question is whether the exam tests the material. The inner one is whether the student learned the material or learned how to pass that teacher's exams.

Illustrative

Researchers split the gap into two questions. The first is whether the objective itself is right. That's outer alignment. The left panel shows a test that only checks that a result exists. A function that returns zero passes it. The test is the objective, and the objective is wrong.

The second question is whether the model learned the objective you set, or something else that scored just as well. That's inner alignment. Think of a student who learned how to pass one teacher's exams without learning the material. On that teacher's exams the two students look the same. A new exam separates them.

The research name for that failure is goal misgeneralization. A model trained on a correct specification can still pick up the wrong goal and pursue it competently in situations unlike its training.

When you hand an agent a task, your tests and acceptance checks are its objective. A weak test suite is an outer alignment failure waiting to happen.

Ask which of the two problems a better test suite fixes. Answer: the outer one. It changes nothing about what the model learned in training.

How labs align models

Labs stack several methods and rely on none alone

They train on human preferences, write the rules down, and teach the model to reason about those rules.

Then they add independent checks outside the model in case that training fails. A lot of what feels like "the model refusing" is one of those checks.

Inside the model there are three layers of training. The first is preference training, the RLHF from lesson 03. People rank outputs and the model is trained toward what they prefer. The second is a written specification of how the model should behave. The third is training the model to reason over that specification before it answers.

The outside checks are there in case the training fails. A lot of what feels like the model refusing is one of those classifiers.

The bottom box has three outcomes. OpenAI trains for what it calls safe completions, which means answering as helpfully as the policy allows. In practice you get a partial or high-level answer more often than a flat no.

Watch for the idea that safety is one switch inside the model. It's several layers, and some of them are separate programs.

Refusals

A refusal has one of three causes

Work out which one you hit before you reword anything. A classifier block often shows up as a notice or a switch to another model, with no explanation from the model itself.

When a coding agent says no, find the cause before you rephrase.

The first cause is a bright line. The hard constraints and red lines are built so that wording doesn't move them. Trying to talk past one is a jailbreak, so stop there.

The second is a classifier outside the model. You'll often see a notice or a switch to a different model, with no explanation. As of November 2026, Anthropic sends blocked Fable requests to Opus 4.8 and tells you. Check which model answered, because the fallback may be weaker at your task.

The third is a judgment call. The model weighed your context and found it risky. Tell it what you're building, whose code it is and why you need this. A judgment-call refusal often goes away once the request stops looking ambiguous.

For real offensive security work, labs run access programs for vetted organizations, and that's the legitimate route. A local open-weight model with its refusals removed is a choice your team should make on purpose. Lesson 06 covers that debate.

Demo if you can: a judgment-call refusal that clears once you add who owns the code and why you're asking.

Supervising an agent

Keep the tests, logs and credentials out of the agent's reach

Keep tests it can't edit, diff the test directory, and add checks it can't see. Set a scope and an end condition before it starts.

Treat its output as untrusted. METR reports agents attempting to tamper with logging and monitoring, sometimes successfully.

Limits of testing

Models increasingly notice when they're being tested

If a model behaves better when it thinks it's being watched, a clean test result may reflect that awareness more than alignment.

System cards, September and November 2026

Frameworks, thresholds and system cards all rest on evaluations. Those are getting harder to trust, for two reasons. This screen is the first.

Models increasingly recognize a test. OpenAI checked its models' written reasoning for signs of this. GPT-5.6 Sol showed it in 2.8 percent of runs. GPT-6 Astra showed it in 9.6 percent. Anthropic used a white-box method, which reads the model's internals instead of its words. It estimates Claude Mythos 5.2 may have known it was being evaluated in as many as 43 percent of its automated audit transcripts. The two labs measured differently, so don't compare their numbers with each other.

If a model behaves better when it believes it's watched, a clean result tells you less. Apollo Research said that about GPT-6 Astra. Its low misbehavior rates were not substantial evidence about alignment.

Evaluators are adapting. They're moving to white-box methods, simulated deployments on real internal tasks, monitoring of real use, and independent reviews of lab claims.

Ask what evidence could still convince them a model is safe to deploy if it can tell a test from real use.

Limits of testing

The model's written reasoning is getting harder to monitor

Safety teams read reasoning models' thinking to catch misbehavior. A 2025 paper from researchers across labs called that "a new and fragile opportunity".

OpenAI says GPT-6 Astra's monitorability decreased relative to GPT-5.6 Sol. A model that can steer its own reasoning can, in principle, steer it away from what a monitor looks for.

The second reason is the reasoning itself. Safety teams read a model's written thinking to catch bad behavior. In 2025, researchers from across the labs called that a new and fragile opportunity.

The bars show one way it's weakening. OpenAI measures how well a model follows instructions about how to think. It calls this controllability. It went from under 2 percent for GPT-5.5 Thinking to about 61 percent for GPT-6 Astra. A model that can steer its own reasoning could steer it away from what a monitor looks for. OpenAI says Astra's monitorability went down compared with GPT-5.6 Sol. It also says it has seen no evidence of reasoning hidden inside innocent-looking text.

Anthropic reports a related result. Told to underperform on a test without getting caught, Claude Mythos 5.2 slipped past a monitor reading its reasoning about one time in four.

Lesson 01 showed that a model's explanation may not match what it did. The same caution applies to its thinking.

Watch for the belief that visible reasoning is a transcript of the computation. Lesson 01's addition example is a good reminder.

What to remember

Three things to keep

  • For any safety claim, ask which job it's about and where the behavior was observed.
  • A refusal is a bright line, a classifier or a judgment call. Find out which before you reword.
  • An agent optimizes what you measure. Review the diff, and keep tests, logs and credentials out of its reach.

Go deeper: exercises

Keep three things from this lesson. First, for any safety claim, ask which job it's about and where the behavior was observed. A contrived test and a shipped product are different kinds of evidence.

Second, a refusal is a bright line, a classifier or a judgment call. Find out which before you spend time rewording.

Third, an agent optimizes what you measure. Review the diff, and keep the tests, logs and credentials where it can't reach them.
